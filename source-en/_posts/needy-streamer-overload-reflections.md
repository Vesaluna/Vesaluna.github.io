> This article contains spoilers for the story and endings of *NEEDY STREAMER OVERLOAD*.

It has been two weeks since I completed the game, after spending eighty hours with it. My first reaction was to write something, but when my fingers touched the keyboard I realised that I had gone so long without writing that I could no longer express my thoughts clearly. Italian had also begun to influence my Chinese sentences, making them long and oddly shaped.

I spent two weeks delaying, revising and returning to this article. It still feels disorderly and awkward, but this is the form in which I can finally bring it to an end.

## Becoming “P-chan”

The game is *NEEDY STREAMER OVERLOAD*. On opening it, a pixel-art desktop modelled after Windows appears, accompanied by simple and cheerful electronic music.

The story belongs to Ame-chan. I am assigned the role of her Producer—“P-chan,” as she calls me—and given the goal of turning her into a streamer with one million followers within thirty days.

The rules are straightforward. Each day is divided into noon, evening and night, and I can assign Ame-chan an activity at each point. These activities change three essential values—stress, affection and mental darkness—which, together with her follower count, determine the ending.

Every choice and every stream leaves a mark not only on Ame-chan herself, but also in the comments posted by her viewers afterwards.

At first I weighed every option carefully. I tried to steer her away from the worst paths and find a direct route to a happy ending. Yet even after reaching one million followers in thirty days, Ame-chan unexpectedly blocked me. That was my first bad ending.

Puzzled, I began another playthrough and tried to avoid whatever mistake I might have made. Several attempts later, ordinary play still led only to endings I found unsatisfying.

## Breaking the porcelain cup called Ame-chan

The game seems to possess a certain malice. It wants the player to make the choices that will gently and gradually shatter the porcelain cup called Ame-chan, then watch the same vessel break again and again in order to collect endings.

As I exhausted the choices and unlocked more outcomes, her fate began to feel entirely determined by numbers. She seemed less like a person entrusted to me than a cold program still running. I withdrew from the role the game had assigned and resolved simply to observe.

As the story continues, Ame-chan occasionally speaks about her past.

She grew up in poverty while her parents remained in constant conflict. School was equally bleak and frightening. Bullied for no reason beyond her appearance, she eventually dropped out, left her hometown and moved alone to a large city.

The grey colour of that childhood became the ground of her inner world, leaving her starved for affection and painfully sensitive. The game returns to this repeatedly. She constantly asks whether P-chan truly loves her, uses social media posts to reveal feelings indirectly, and finds that intimacy sharply reduces her stress. Each detail exposes profound insecurity in her relationships.

Life in the city was harsh for someone alone and empty-handed. After discovering the internet, she was quickly absorbed by it. For the first time she felt that she could be loved, that someone might pay attention to her and offer comfort. At the beginning of the game, she therefore chooses streaming in the hope of receiving still more attention, devotion and love.

She writes a persona for herself and becomes OMGkawaiiAngel, an angel of the online world. Commemorative streams appear as her audience grows. They suggest that beyond satisfying her own desires, she also wants to become an angel capable of comforting people who resemble her.

## “Dear God”

Streaming brings many kinds of pressure, especially when the goal is an almost impossible one million followers in thirty days. Ame-chan already has serious psychological difficulties, and the “medication” action allows her to overdose. It is one of the few ways to reduce stress quickly, but it also increases mental darkness dramatically.

In one playthrough, when her darkness reaches eighty, the final drug appears: the “Magic Paper,” or LSD. After taking it five times, she discovers that her world is only a game and becomes aware of the presence behind P-chan. She then pleads with the player:

> Dear God, I know you've always been watching me. I've tried so hard. Please, won't you let me feel some warmth?

The word “needy” in the game's earlier English title, *Needy Girl Overdose*, carries two meanings. Ame-chan longs for a warmth she has never known, while I, as the player, appear to possess the power to perform certain miracles.

Yet her plea feels like an accusation. It makes me consider the relationship between human beings and the internet.

In one ending, Ame-chan travels at night to the Galactic Railroad station and boards a train racing through the stars, an allusion to Kenji Miyazawa's famous tale *Night on the Galactic Railroad*. Surrounded by starlight, she asks during an imaginary stream:

> If God really exists, then if we keep doing good, will He take us to heaven?

By the end of the game, however, she has not reached the heaven she hoped for. Her god truly exists, but remains high above, doing nothing but watching.

## Internet Overdose

Another ending that affected me deeply is called “Internet Overdose.” Ame-chan reaches maximum stress twice. During a stream, hostile comments make her physically ill and she vomits.

The bloodied bait falls into the water and immediately draws a school of sharks. The more fiercely she struggles, the more violently they bite.

Her personal information is exposed. Unexplained cameras appear near her home. Abuse and mockery cover the internet, placing her under such pressure that she begins to hallucinate. In this ending, Ame-chan dies by suicide while viewers fill the stream with jeering messages.

## Day 0 and a future in reality

After twenty-two endings, a save file called “Day 0” appears on the title screen. The player has no control after entering it. Ame-chan sends messages herself and says she wants to reach one million followers through her own effort.

Although she begins confused and repeatedly stumbles, she eventually reaches the goal on her own.

In the final scene, Ame-chan leaves the screen, saying that she is going to seize a new future. I can finally open the previously obscured “secret.txt” and learn that P-chan was only her fantasy. In every earlier ending she depended upon that fantasy. At the end, she breaks away from it and obtains what she wanted in reality.

There is no usual completion message after the true ending. The only way to close the game is to find a button marked “Good night.”

Perhaps it is a sign that we, too, should turn our eyes back towards reality.

The internet can offer comfort like the only blue butterfly fluttering through the dark, drawing us after it until we lose ourselves. But life is built in reality. When the melody of the music box stops, the butterfly disappears into shadow and the dream ends.

True happiness cannot be requested from a god or obtained by relying on fantasy. It can only be glimpsed and caught while we stumble forward through the real world.

I hope that I, too, can one day write as calmly as Ame-chan:

> My happiness is neither false nor frightening.<br>
> Ame-chan's life had meaning too.<br>
> I truly lived.
